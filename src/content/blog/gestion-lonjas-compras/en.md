Managing fish market purchasing efficiently means that every sales note received at auction becomes usable information — price, batch, species, weight, and supplier — without anyone having to retype it into another system. The usual bottleneck isn't the fish market itself, but what happens afterward: manually transcribing dozens of lines from a paper or PDF document into an ERP or a spreadsheet, with the error risk that involves and the time it takes away from tasks that genuinely require human judgment, like negotiating price or deciding what to buy.

A company that buys daily at one or more fish markets — and often also from direct suppliers and toll processors — needs that information to enter the system once and, from there, automatically feed stock, production, and, later, the margin calculation for each sales order. When that doesn't happen, fish market purchasing and internal company management work as two separate worlds that someone has to reconcile by hand every day.

## What documentation each fish market purchase generates

Each fish market purchase typically generates at least:

- A **sales note or fish market invoice**, breaking down species, weight, price per kilo, and batch for each auctioned lot.
- A **delivery note**, if transport to the warehouse is handled by a third party.
- A **catch certificate**, when the species or the product's destination requires it.

Each of these documents carries data that's later needed in production (weight and species), traceability (batch and origin), and purchasing (price and supplier). If each document is filed separately without connecting that data, the company loses half the value of having it in the first place.

## The real cost of transcribing documents by hand

Manually transcribing a fish market sales note isn't just slow — it's a point where a good share of any company's stock and traceability errors creep in: a mistyped weight, a batch confused with another, a price transcribed with a decimal point in the wrong place. When a company buys at several fish markets and from several suppliers on the same day, the volume of documents that need manual entry grows quickly, and so does the chance that something gets written down wrong, or simply doesn't get recorded in time.

## Fish market prices and margin: why timely updates matter

Fish market prices vary by auction, and that purchase price is the basis for later calculating the margin on every sales order. If the real price of each batch takes hours or days to show up in the system — because someone has to transcribe it first — any order closed with that product during that time is being calculated against an outdated cost. This isn't an abstract accounting precision issue: it's real margin being lost or overstated without anyone noticing until month-end close.

## Purchasing beyond the fish market: suppliers and toll processors

Purchasing management in this sector isn't limited to the fish market. A frozen goods company typically combines:

- Direct fish market purchases.
- Regular suppliers with agreed terms and payment schedules.
- Toll processors, who receive the company's own raw material, transform it, and return it — a flow that has to be tracked and settled separately, without losing traceability of the original batch.

Each of these channels generates its own documentation and its own payment schedules, and all of them should live in the same purchasing system to get a real picture of what's been bought, from whom, and at what price, at any given moment.
